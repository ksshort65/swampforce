HOW A NARRATIVE IS BUILT: THE PSYCHOLOGY BEHIND THE HEADLINES

Every entry on this page is a claim about President Trump or his administration that was later corrected, retracted, settled in court, or shown false by the record. This page explains how those claims work on the mind, and why they keep getting made.

WHAT PSYCHOLOGICAL WARFARE IS
Psychological warfare means using information to shape how a population thinks, feels, and acts, without changing the facts on the ground. The modern term was set out by Paul M. A. Linebarger in Psychological Warfare (1948): in the narrow sense it is propaganda aimed at an enemy, backed by other operations; propaganda itself is organized persuasion by non-violent means. The scholarly study of wartime persuasion goes back further. Harold D. Lasswell's Propaganda Technique in the World War (1927) mapped how governments in World War I managed opinions through symbols, stories, and suggestion. Cold War research then treated mass persuasion as a standing tool of statecraft. The same toolkit travels easily into peacetime politics and media. RAND analysts Christopher Paul and Miriam Matthews later described a high-volume model they called the "firehose of falsehood": many channels, rapid repetition, little concern for consistency or truth, used to overwhelm rather than persuade carefully (Paul & Matthews, 2016). Domestic political coverage can run the same way—flood the zone, repeat the charge, bury the fix—whether anyone planned it that way or not. The tools are still repetition, framing, selective editing, emotional triggers, and timing. When newsrooms and campaigns use them, the target is the public's sense of what is real.

THE SCIENCE BEHIND IT
The effects below are among the best documented. The next sections name gaslighting and each deception method used in the tables; those entries point back here instead of repeating the same studies.
- Repetition creates belief. Psychologists call this the illusory truth effect. A statement people hear again and again starts to feel true, even when they already know better (Hasher, Goldstein & Toppino, 1977; Fazio et al., 2015).
- Corrections don't erase the first impression. Research on the continued influence effect shows that people keep relying on false information after it has been retracted (Johnson & Seifert, 1994; Lewandowsky et al., 2012). A front-page accusation and a correction buried inside the paper don't cancel each other out.
- Falsehoods travel faster than the truth. An MIT study of Twitter found that false news stories were 70 percent more likely to be retweeted than true ones and reached people about six times faster (Vosoughi, Roy & Aral, Science, 2018).
- Outrage spreads. Each moral or emotional word in a post raised how widely it was shared by about 20 percent (Brady et al., PNAS, 2017). Posts attacking the other political side were far more likely to be shared than any other kind (Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden, PNAS, 2021).
- Framing changes meaning. Cutting a sentence short, dropping the context, or putting a new caption on an old photo can flip what the audience concludes, even when every word shown is technically accurate (see Framing and Contextomy in the methods glossary below).

WHY YOU NEVER SAW THE CORRECTION
A front-page falsehood and a quiet fix do not cancel each other. The continued influence effect shows that people keep relying on retracted information after it has been corrected (Johnson & Seifert, 1994). False stories also travel farther and faster than true ones (Vosoughi, Roy & Aral, Science, 2018). In practice, many corrections arrive as a buried editor's note, a deleted tweet, or no correction at all—only a later fact-check. In this site's audit of 252 cases, 159 were never corrected by the original pusher (152 fact-checked only; 7 with no fact-check on file); 64 carried an appended correction line; 8 used an editor's note; 5 followed a legal threat or settlement; and 2 ran an on-air correction. See Correction_Visibility on each row below. Until a correction matches the placement and reach of the original claim, most voters never see it.

GASLIGHTING
The word comes from Patrick Hamilton's play Gas Light (premiered 1938) and from the better-known 1944 film Gaslight, in which a husband dims the lights and insists his wife is imagining it so she will doubt her own mind. Clinicians later used the idea for intimate psychological abuse; Robin Stern's The Gaslight Effect (2007) brought the term to a wide audience. Sociologist Paige L. Sweet argued in the American Sociological Review that gaslighting is also a social power tactic: making someone seem or feel "crazy" so their account of reality is discredited (Sweet, 2019). In politics and media, the pattern is the same move at public scale. A claim is pushed hard. When the record shows it was false, the correction is quiet, late, or framed as a quibble—while the original story is still repeated, mocked as a "conspiracy theory" if you noticed the fix, or treated as if it never happened. Viewers are told they misremembered the tape, overreacted to the headline, or cannot trust what they saw. That is political gaslighting: denying documented reality, insisting a debunked story was basically true, and punishing people for keeping the receipts. Many cases below end with a buried editor's note or a same-day apology that never catches the first blast. The science of continued influence (see above) is why that works.

THE METHODS, AND THE RESEARCH THAT NAMED THEM
Every "Type of Deception" tag in the tables maps to one of the entries below. Closely related tags are grouped. Methods already covered under The Science Behind It are named here once and pointed back there.
- Contextomy / quoting out of context: Cutting words so the remainder means something the speaker did not say. Communication researcher Matthew S. McGlone called this "contextomy" and showed how the truncated quote sticks even after the full context is restored (McGlone, 2005). In these cases: misquote / truncation tags, including verdict labels and clipped soundbites.
- Paltering: Misleading with statements that are technically true. Rogers, Zeckhauser, Gino, Norton, and Schweitzer showed people often prefer this to outright lies, while targets still feel deceived (Rogers et al., 2017). In these cases: true-but-tilted claims that omit the decisive fact.
- Framing: Choosing which slice of reality to highlight so the audience reaches a preferred conclusion. Tversky and Kahneman showed decision frames change choices (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981); Robert Entman defined media framing as selecting and highlighting some aspects of a story while leaving others out (Entman, 1993). In these cases: premature "proven" framing, inflammatory false premise, and other frame-driven tags.
- Agenda-setting: News does not just tell people what to think—it tells them what to think about. McCombs and Shaw's study of the 1968 election showed issue emphasis in the press matched what voters called important (McCombs & Shaw, 1972). In these cases: wall-to-wall treatment of a thin or false charge as the story of the day.
- Priming: Once an issue is on the mental agenda, people use it as a yardstick for leaders and opponents. Iyengar and Kinder demonstrated this with television news experiments (Iyengar & Kinder, News That Matters, 1987). In these cases: coverage that keeps one damaging theme active so every new headline is judged against it.
- Anchoring / first impressions: Early numbers and first stories pull later judgments toward them (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974). See also The Science Behind It on first impressions and corrections.
- Illusory truth effect: Repetition makes claims feel true. See The Science Behind It (Hasher, Goldstein & Toppino, 1977).
- Continued influence effect: Retracted falsehoods still shape later thinking. See The Science Behind It (Johnson & Seifert, 1994).
- Misleading statistics: Charts, averages, and comparisons that are numerically true but practically false. Darrell Huff popularized the playbook in How to Lie with Statistics (Huff, 1954). In these cases: misleading statistic and false absolute comparison tags.
- Omission / selective reporting: Leaving out the fact that would change the meaning. Related to paltering; Rogers and colleagues distinguish this passive form from active false statements (Rogers et al., 2017). In these cases: omitted context tags.
- False attribution of words or intent: Putting words, motives, or positions on someone without a fair basis, including thin anonymous sourcing treated as proof. In these cases: false attribution of words/intent tags.
- Fabrication and retracted invention: Invented events, documents, posts, or quotes later walked back. In these cases: fabrication, retracted invention, and stolen-satire tags.
- False photo or video attribution, cheap fakes, and deepfakes: Real footage labeled as the wrong event, lightly edited "cheap fakes," or machine-made deepfakes. Paris and Donovan mapped this spectrum (Paris & Donovan, Data & Society, 2019). Chesney and Citron warned of a "liar's dividend": once fakes are common, genuine evidence can be dismissed as fake too (Chesney & Citron, 2019). In these cases: false photo/video attribution and related fabrication tags.
- Policy-scope inflation: Stretching a real action into a much larger or opposite policy ("ending" a program that was trimmed, "banning" a group covered by a narrower rule). Built from framing plus omission. In these cases: policy-scope inflation tags.
- Premature "proven" framing: Treating an allegation as settled fact before evidence can bear the weight. A framing and agenda-setting failure. In these cases: premature "proven" framing tags.
- Confirmation bias: Seeking and overweighting information that fits what you already believe (Nickerson, 1998). Helps explain why same-leaning newsrooms under-check matching stories.
- Motivated reasoning: Directional goals—wanting a candidate to win or lose—steer what counts as convincing evidence (Kunda, 1990). Helps explain why corrections from "the other side" bounce off.
- Moral-emotional contagion: Moral and emotional wording makes claims travel farther in social networks. See The Science Behind It (Brady et al., 2017).

WHY POLITICIANS AND NETWORKS DO IT
The incentives are simple. Outrage brings ratings, clicks, and donations. A damaging story about a political opponent moves polls and votes. Being first pays better than being right, and a correction days later costs almost nothing. Inside newsrooms and parties where everyone thinks alike, a story that confirms what they already believe gets less scrutiny. None of this needs a central plan to look like one. When many outlets and officials share the same incentives, they all push in the same direction.

WHO PAID FOR IT, AND WHY
Some of these narratives came with a price tag. The best documented is the Steele dossier. The Clinton campaign and the Democratic National Committee paid for it through their law firm, Perkins Coie, which hired the research firm Fusion GPS. Fusion GPS then hired former British spy Christopher Steele. The campaign and the DNC reported those payments as legal expenses. In 2022 the Federal Election Commission fined them $113,000 for hiding what the money actually bought. That paid-for opposition research then fed FBI surveillance applications and years of wall-to-wall coverage. The rest of the money is less hidden and follows the business model. Networks and newspapers sell attention to advertisers and subscribers, and coverage of President Trump reliably drew record audiences. Political campaigns and committees raise donations with fear and outrage about their opponent. The motive is the same for all of them: power and money. A damaging story helps win elections, keeps a party's base angry and giving, and keeps the audience watching. And when the story turns out to be false, there's almost never a cost that comes close to what it earned.

WHY THE HOSTILITY RUNS SO DEEP
Donald Trump came to Washington as an outsider who owed nothing to the people who normally run it. He challenged the leadership of both parties, the permanent bureaucracy, the foreign-policy establishment, and the national press, and he said openly that he meant to take power away from them. People and institutions whose status, careers, and income depend on that system saw him as a threat in a way they never saw an ordinary opponent. The hostility also became personal on both sides. He mocked reporters and politicians by name and called the press "the enemy of the people," and many of them came to see stopping him as a moral duty rather than a political disagreement. Researchers have measured how deep this kind of feeling now runs. Political scientists Shanto Iyengar and Sean Westwood found that Americans' hostility toward the other party now exceeds racial bias on several measures (American Journal of Political Science, 2015). Once people see an opponent as a threat to everything they value, bending the truth to stop him starts to feel justified, and the damage to public trust, to voters, and to the country becomes a cost they are willing to accept. The cases on this page show where that thinking leads.

WHEN NEWS BECAME OPINION
News used to lead with what happened. Over the past three decades it has led more often with how the speaker feels about what happened. RAND's quantitative study News in a Digital Age (Kavanagh et al., 2019) measured that shift across print, broadcast, cable, and online: content moved from more-objective event- and context-based reporting toward subjectivity, argumentation, advocacy, and emotional appeals. RAND found the change across outlets generally; it was modest in newspapers, stronger in television news, and sharpest when broadcast news was compared with prime-time cable programming—where opinion shows often sit in the same brands as the news. An earlier RAND report, Truth Decay (Kavanagh & Rich, 2018), named the wider pattern: a blurring of the line between opinion and fact, and a rise in the volume of opinion relative to fact. Engagement rewards the same turn. Rathje, Van Bavel, and van der Linden (PNAS, 2021) found that posts attacking the other political side were far more likely to be shared than other kinds of posts—so animosity travels farther than neutral reporting. When the feed prefers argument and emotion, audiences receive less verified event and more branded outrage.

Our view: Americans are no longer given the news. They are given hate-filled opinion dressed as news.

WHO'S IN THE NEWSROOM
The people who write and edit the news are not a cross-section of the country. Indiana University's long-running American Journalist survey found in 2022 that about 36 percent of U.S. journalists identified as Democrats and only 3.4 percent as Republicans, with about 52 percent calling themselves independents (Willnat, Weaver & Wilhoit, The American Journalist Under Attack, 2022). In 2013 the same project had put Democrats at 28 percent and Republicans at 7 percent, so the Republican share roughly halved in a decade while Democrats rose. In the 2016 presidential race, a Center for Public Integrity analysis of federal campaign filings found that more than 96 percent of the money donated by people working in journalism to the Clinton or Trump campaigns went to Clinton (Center for Public Integrity, 2016). Coverage of Trump's first months in office tracked in the same direction. Harvard's Shorenstein Center, led by Thomas Patterson, found that of news reports with a clear tone during Trump's first 100 days, 80 percent were negative and 20 percent positive; on CNN and NBC the negative-to-positive ratio was about 13 to 1 (Shorenstein Center, 2017). An earlier Pew Research Center survey of national journalists found a similar tilt in self-described ideology: 34 percent called themselves liberal and only 7 percent conservative, compared with 19 percent liberal and about a third conservative among the public (Pew Research Center, 2004).

Our view: Taken together with the documented cases below, the owner of this site believes these repeated methods of deception were not a string of honest mistakes. They look like an intentional effort to sway voters against Donald Trump and his administration.

THE GREAT AMERICAN BETRAYAL
Our view: Deliberately deceiving voters at scale should be treated as a crime against self-government. It is a betrayal of the republic, because free elections assume that citizens can give informed consent. When false claims are pushed, amplified, and left standing long after the record has corrected them, that consent is poisoned.

Hannah Arendt named what is at stake. In The Origins of Totalitarianism (enlarged 2nd ed., 1958; the passage comes from "Ideology and Terror," first published in The Review of Politics in 1953) she wrote: "The ideal subject of totalitarian rule is not the convinced Nazi or the convinced Communist, but people for whom the distinction between fact and fiction (i.e., the reality of experience) and the distinction between true and false (i.e., the standards of thought) no longer exist." In "Truth and Politics" (The New Yorker, 1967) she argued that factual truth is the ground political opinion stands on—"Freedom of opinion is a farce unless factual information is guaranteed and the facts themselves are not in dispute"—and that factual truth "is always in danger of being maneuvered out of the world not only for a time but, potentially, forever." In "Lying in Politics" (1971), reflecting on the Pentagon Papers, she found that the policy of lying was "chiefly if not exclusively destined for domestic consumption, for propaganda at home and especially for the purpose of deceiving Congress." In a 1974 interview with Roger Errera, later published in The New York Review of Books (1978), she warned: "a people that no longer can believe anything cannot make up its mind. It is deprived not only of its capacity to act but also of its capacity to think and to judge."

Current U.S. law protects much false speech: in United States v. Alvarez (2012) the Supreme Court struck down the Stolen Valor Act, and the plurality wrote that "falsity alone may not suffice to bring the speech outside the First Amendment." Under New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964), a public official suing over a falsehood about official conduct must prove "actual malice"—knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard of the truth. This section is not a claim about what the courts already punish. It is an argument about what accountability should become: if a free people cannot tell fact from fiction, they cannot govern themselves.

Our view: Congress, which writes the rules for itself, has betrayed voters by refusing to police its own members' false statements. In this record alone, 48 cases were pushed by a sitting Representative, Senator, Speaker, or party leader in the House or Senate. The Speech or Debate Clause (U.S. Const. art. I, § 6) shields members over legislative acts—floor speech, committee work, and related deliberative acts—but it did not cover a senator's private publication of the Pentagon Papers, which "was in no way essential to the deliberations of the Senate" (Gravel v. United States, 1972), or a senator's newsletters and press release (Hutchinson v. Proxmire, 1979). Even so, the House rarely uses its own discipline: the House Historian lists six expulsions and 29 censures in its entire history. On June 21, 2023, the House censured Rep. Adam Schiff over Russia-related claims (H. Res. 521), 213–209 with 6 present: every yea was Republican and every nay was Democrat—an example of a rarely used tool applied along party lines, not a standing accuracy rule.

WHY IT MATTERS
When a nation is told false things about its elected president again and again, trust in its institutions wears down. Voters make decisions based on bad information, and the presidency itself is weakened at home and abroad. Each case below lists when it started, when it ended if it ever did, who pushed it, and a link to the record that set it straight. Judge for yourself.
