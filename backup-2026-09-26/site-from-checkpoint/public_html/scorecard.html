What drove it, era by era
1790–1812$71.1 million → $45.2 million

The founding debt. Congress took on the Revolutionary War debt. The Funding Act of 1790 swapped the old debts for new federal bonds. By 1811 the debt had been cut. Funding Act of 1790 (Treasury history) ↗ · Revolutionary War debt (Treasury) ↗

1812–1816$45.2 million → $127.3 million

War of 1812. The war was paid for mainly with borrowed money. Treasury history of the debt ↗

1816–1835$127.3 million → $33,733

Paid off. Land sales and budget cuts brought the debt to almost zero. By January 1835 all interest-bearing debt was paid off, the only time it has happened. Treasury: debt briefly eliminated in 1835 ↗ · Treasury history of the debt ↗

1860–1866$64.8 million → $2.77 billion

Civil War. Debt grew more than 40 times over. The Legal Tender Act of 1862 authorized $150 million in notes and $500 million in bonds. Legal Tender Act, 1862 (Treasury history) ↗ · Civil War debt (Treasury) ↗

1916–1919$3.61 billion → $27.39 billion

World War I. Congress authorized the first Liberty Loan of $5 billion. Liberty Loan Act, 1917 (Treasury history) ↗

1930–1940$16.19 billion → $42.97 billion

Depression and New Deal. Hard times pushed deficits up as public works were funded. By 1933 the debt topped $22 billion. Savings Bonds went on sale in 1935. Treasury history of the debt ↗

1941–1946$48.96 billion → $269.42 billion

World War II. About $211 billion of the war’s estimated $323 billion cost was borrowed. Debt held by the public peaked at 106% of the economy in 1946. War financing (Treasury history) ↗ · CBO: 106% of GDP in 1946 ↗

1946–1965$269.42 billion → $317.27 billion

Postwar years. Budgets ran surpluses from 1946 to 1949. Debt rose only modestly, and after inflation its real value fell. Treasury history of the debt ↗

1965–1980$317.27 billion → $907.70 billion

Great Society, Medicare, Vietnam. Medicare and Medicaid became law on July 30, 1965. The Vietnam War and Great Society programs raised deficits. 1969 was the last surplus until 1998. Medicare and Medicaid Act, 1965 (National Archives) ↗ · Treasury history of the debt ↗

1981–1990$997.86 billion → $3.23 trillion

1980s: tax cuts and defense buildup. The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 cut taxes. Borrowing paid for a military buildup. The debt more than tripled from 1980 to 1990. Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 (H.R. 4242) ↗ · Treasury history of the debt ↗

1990–2001$3.23 trillion → $5.81 trillion

1990s: budget deals. Congress passed budget reconciliation acts in 1990 and 1993. The budget went back into the black in 1998. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1990 (P.L. 101-508) ↗ · Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1993 (P.L. 103-66) ↗ · Treasury: back in the black in 1998 ↗

2001–2008$5.81 trillion → $10.02 trillion

2001 to 2008: tax cuts, two wars, a drug benefit. Congress passed tax cuts in 2001 and 2003, authorized force in Afghanistan (2001) and Iraq (2002), and added Medicare drug coverage (2003). Treasury lists the Afghanistan and Iraq wars among the big debt spikes. EGTRRA 2001 (P.L. 107-16) ↗ · JGTRRA 2003 (P.L. 108-27) ↗ · Use of force, 2001 (P.L. 107-40) ↗ · Iraq resolution, 2002 (P.L. 107-243) ↗ · Medicare Part D, 2003 (P.L. 108-173) ↗ · Treasury: debt spikes ↗

2008–2016$10.02 trillion → $19.57 trillion

2008 crisis and recovery. The 2008 bank rescue (TARP) and the 2009 Recovery Act passed during the Great Recession, one of the big debt spikes Treasury names. Emergency Economic Stabilization Act, TARP (P.L. 110-343) ↗ · American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (P.L. 111-5) ↗ · Treasury: debt spikes ↗

2016–2019$19.57 trillion → $22.72 trillion

2017 tax law. The 2017 tax law (TCJA) lowered individual income tax rates. The 2025 reconciliation act made those lower rates permanent, per CBO. Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (P.L. 115-97) ↗ · CBO on the 2017 and 2025 laws ↗

2019–2021$22.72 trillion → $28.43 trillion

COVID-19. The CARES Act (2020) and the American Rescue Plan (2021) passed. Treasury says spending rose about 50% from FY2019 to FY2021. CARES Act (P.L. 116-136) ↗ · American Rescue Plan Act (P.L. 117-2) ↗ · Treasury: spending up about 50% ↗

2021–now$28.43 trillion → $40.07 trillion

2022 to today: interest. Net interest rose from $970 billion in 2025 to over $1.0 trillion in 2026, CBO projects. CBO estimates the 2025 reconciliation act (P.L. 119-21) added $4.7 trillion to deficits over 2026–2035. CBO Budget and Economic Outlook, Feb 2026 ↗ · Debt to the Penny ↗

How Congress let it happen

FY1997 was the last time all regular spending bills were enacted by the October 1 start of the fiscal year. Since then Congress has relied on stopgap bills (continuing resolutions) to keep the government funded past October 1. CRS, Omnibus Appropriations ↗

From 1982 through FY2024, 36 omnibus spending packages were enacted, carrying 276 of the 525 possible regular spending bills. CRS ↗

Funding lapsed and the government shut down in October and November 2025. CBO, Feb 2026 ↗

Looking ahead, CBO says rising spending on Social Security and Medicare and rising net interest drive outlays up. By 2056 net interest (6.9% of GDP) would exceed Social Security (6.0%) and Medicare (5.5%). CBO long-term outlook ↗
